The lighthouse keeper had kept the same log for thirty-one years, and the entries had grown shorter as the years went on. In the first volume, from the spring he arrived, a single day might run to two pages: the colour of the water, the names of the boats that passed, what the gulls were doing, whether the bread had risen. By the fourth volume he had settled into a shorthand that only he could read, and by the ninth he was writing three or four words a day and considering that sufficient.

His replacement, a young woman from the mainland who had trained on the automated stations further north, found the volumes stacked in the oil room in no particular order. She had expected to throw them out. Instead she spent her first winter reading them backwards, from the terse late entries toward the expansive early ones, which had the effect of watching a man slowly become more interested in the world rather than less.

What struck her most was not the weather, though he had recorded it faithfully, nor the shipping, though he had recorded that too. It was a running argument he seemed to be having with himself about whether the light mattered. In the second volume he wrote that the beam was the only thing standing between the rocks and the ships, and that this was a plain fact requiring no elaboration. In the sixth he noted that radar had made him ornamental. In the eighth, after a night when a trawler had lost power and drifted for six hours within sight of the rocks, he wrote a single line: they saw it and turned, and I do not know whether the radar was working.

She thought about that line often during her own first year. The station was scheduled for automation within the decade; everyone said so, and everyone had been saying so for twenty years. The equipment she tended was newer than his had been, and the fog signal no longer required anyone to be awake at three in the morning. Yet she found herself awake at three in the morning anyway, more often than the schedule required, watching the beam sweep out over water she could not see.

In the spring she began her own log. She had intended to keep it in the modern format, the one the authority had issued, with its boxes for visibility and wind speed and its small square for remarks. After a month she abandoned the form and bought a bound notebook in the town on the mainland, and began writing about the colour of the water, and the names of the boats, and what the gulls were doing.
